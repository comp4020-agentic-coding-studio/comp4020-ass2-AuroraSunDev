**Structural question — how does the outer layer bear, distribute and recover
from load?**

Held at one end, an outer layer behaves like a beam: it spans, it deflects, and
past some point it stops coming back. This week measures that.

## The outer layer as structure

A beam under load carries compression along its upper surface and tension along
its lower one, and fails when either exceeds what the material can bear. An
outer layer is a poor beam — inconsistent, porous, and changing by the minute —
but it fails in the same recognisable order: deflection first, then a hinge at
the point of greatest bending, then separation.

The useful distinction is between **elastic** behaviour, where the layer
returns to its original geometry once the load is removed, and **plastic**
behaviour, where it does not. Structures are usually designed to stay in the
first regime. Lunch routinely does not.

## Investigation

On the horizontal compression bench, support a specimen at both ends and load
it at the centre. Record deflection at three load steps, then remove the load
and record what returns. Repeat with the span shortened.

The comparison to make is not "which specimen is stronger" but *at which load
step the behaviour changed*, and whether shortening the span moved it.

## Failure condition

The layer forms a permanent hinge and does not recover its original geometry
when unloaded. Record the load step at which this first occurred, not merely
that it occurred.

## What leaves the session

A **Compression Record**: a labelled specimen, the span and support positions,
three load steps with deflection, and a statement of what recovered and what
did not. Note any measurement you are unsure of — an uncertain reading recorded
as uncertain is evidence; an uncertain reading recorded as fact is not.
