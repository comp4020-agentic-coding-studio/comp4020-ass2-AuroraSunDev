The lecture the semester is built around, and the only one in the course that
carries a full deck. Twelve slides move from the claim in the title to the
Component Test the claim is machinery for.

The page that holds it is a slide stage with the complete transcript beneath:
every figure is described in words, every slide has its own address, and the
deck works with the keyboard alone.

## What the lecture argues

Moisture is treated as a load. It is declared, applied under a stated
condition, and measured — the same discipline Week 2 applied to a mechanical
load, moved onto something that acts over time rather than at an instant.

The consequence is a change in what counts as a record. A time-dependent
failure cannot be described by an observation taken at one moment, so every
reading this week carries the elapsed time it was taken at.

## The one piece of established physics

The deck leans on the Lucas–Washburn relation for capillary penetration of a
porous medium, which gives penetration depth proportional to the square root of
elapsed time. It holds under assumptions a real outer layer does not satisfy: a
rigid, uniform, non-swelling pore network and a constant contact angle. Both
original papers are linked above.

That is why slide 8 asks students to read the *shape* of the advance rather
than to fit a curve to it. The prediction that the front slows is robust; the
numbers are not.

## What is not established

Every measurement quoted in the deck is an illustrative course value, and each
slide showing one says so on its face.

The four failure families — Slip, Soak, Crush and Splay — are this course's own
analytical categories, designed to make failures comparable across twelve
weeks. Slide 11 says so explicitly. They are not a recognised professional
classification and should not be cited as one.
