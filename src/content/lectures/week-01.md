The first sixty minutes. It sets up the proposition the semester tests, and it
sets up the standard of evidence the semester marks against.

## What the course claims

A sandwich is a temporary structure whose failure is eaten. That sentence is
doing three things: it asserts that the assembly carries load, that its
condition changes over time, and that the usual evidence of its failure is
destroyed before anyone examines it.

Each of the three has a consequence. Because it carries load, it has a load
path worth drawing. Because it changes, a record needs a time on it. And
because the evidence is normally destroyed, this course photographs,
dimensions and annotates specimens that a diner would simply eat.

## Why the definition is the first problem

Every criterion you might use to decide what counts draws its boundary
somewhere inconvenient — enclosure excludes the open-faced assembly, a count
of outer layers excludes the wrap, and use admits things nothing else does.

The lecture does not settle this. It establishes that a criterion has to be
stated before a classification means anything, because the criterion decides
which failures are visible at all. A definition that excludes the wrap cannot
describe how a wrap fails.

## What evidence will mean

Three categories, kept apart from the first week to the last: what was
observed, what is inferred from it, and what remains unknown. Most of the marks
in this course are for keeping the three in their own columns.

Week 1's own activity is a classification field note, and the Sandwich Field
Log is built from four of them.
