**Weight 20% · Capability: Isolate · Due 9 April 2027, end of Week 6**

> Isolate one structural variable and test it under at least three conditions,
> using a protocol that would survive being handed to someone else.

## Task at a glance

- **Mode** — individual.
- **Format** — a test report: question, declared variable, protocol,
  comparison table, annotated evidence, interpretation and limitations.
- **Scale** — one variable, three or more conditions, plus a control where the
  question supports one.
- **Evidence** — physical specimens, non-food analogues, or the supplied
  measurement packs.
- **Route** — one file, submitted to the SLOP2895 submission point before the
  deadline.

## The brief

The Field Log recorded assemblies as they were found, with their histories
already written into them and mostly unreadable. This task removes that
ambiguity by building the specimen instead of meeting it. You choose one
variable — stiffness category, moisture placement, interface grip, compression,
layer thickness — and you change only that, across conditions you declare in
advance.

The declared problem is that an assembly has too many variables to argue about
honestly. Any observed difference between two whole sandwiches has a dozen
candidate causes, and a comparison that changes three things at once cannot
attribute the result to any of them. Isolation is the move that makes a
difference mean something: hold everything else as close to constant as you can,
vary one thing deliberately, and the result belongs to that thing rather than to
the assembly in general.

A protocol is the second half of that argument. A procedure that only its
author can execute is not a protocol. Write it so that another student in this
course could run it from your document alone, get a result, and compare it with
yours — and then say plainly what your test could not hold
constant, because that list is part of the finding and not a confession
appended to it.

A test whose result contradicts what you expected is not a failed submission. A
well-designed, well-documented test that returns an inconvenient result marks
higher than a loose one that returns a tidy story.

## Required submission

1. A focused research question, narrow enough that three conditions can answer
   it.
2. One declared variable under investigation, with the property being varied
   defined in measurable terms.
3. A repeatable test protocol: specimen preparation as a structural
   specification, the fixed conditions, the loading or exposure applied, what is
   measured, and how many times.
4. A comparison table covering every condition, including the control where one
   is used.
5. Annotated evidence — photographs, diagrams or measurement records — keyed to
   the conditions in the table.
6. An interpretation and limitations section.

## Recommended process

1. **Declare** the question and the single variable before you touch a
   specimen, and write down what you expect to happen.
2. **Classify** the conditions you will test, and state which one is the control
   or why the question does not support one.
3. **Document** the protocol in full, including every quantity you intend to
   hold constant across conditions.
4. **Test** each condition in the same way, in a stated order, and record what
   you did differently whenever you were forced to deviate.
5. **Measure** the response at the same point and in the same units for every
   condition.
6. **Compare** the conditions against each other rather than against your
   expectation.
7. **Revise** the protocol once, run at least one condition again under the
   revised version, and report both runs.
8. **Defend** the interpretation by naming the result that would have overturned
   it.

## Evidence standard

Keep the layers of your report separable. **Observation** is the measurement as
recorded, including the ones that disagree with the others. **Inference** is the
claim you draw about the variable, and it must point to the specific conditions
that support it. **Uncertainty** is the spread across repeats, the resolution of
your measurement method, and the readings you are not confident in — recorded as
uncertain rather than quietly dropped. **Limitation** is what the protocol could
not control: specimens that were not identical to begin with, ambient conditions
that drifted, a measurement taken by eye.

Three conditions with one honest repeat and a stated measurement resolution is
stronger evidence than six conditions measured once each. Fabricated or
reconstructed measurements are not evidence and are marked as absent.

## Equivalent participation

Three routes are available, and all three assess the same outcome:

- **Physical specimen** — test real materials, with no requirement to consume
  anything you test.
- **Non-food analogue** — sponge, foam, cardboard, clay or textile standing in
  for the layer whose property you are varying. This route is often the better
  engineering choice here: analogues are more uniform between specimens, which
  is exactly what an isolation test wants, and they survive repeated destructive
  testing.
- **Supplied evidence** — the course measurement packs, which carry enough
  conditions to support a comparison.

No route loses marks, and the marking criteria are identical across all three.
See [the participation policies](/policies/).

## Safety, cost and waste

No expensive or specialist materials are required, and premium ingredients earn
nothing. Your protocol must justify the quantity of material it consumes; use
the smallest specimen that can answer the question, and prefer a non-food
analogue where the test is destructive and repeated. Tested material must never
be represented as safe to eat. Full detail is on [the policies page](/policies/).
