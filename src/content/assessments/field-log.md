**Weight 15% · Capability: Observe · Due 12 March 2027, end of Week 3**

> Record four specimens as structures, keeping what you observed apart from
> what you concluded from it.

## Task at a glance

- **Mode** — individual.
- **Format** — a four-entry observation sheet, one specimen per entry, in a
  single document.
- **Scale** — four specimens; no more than 250 words of analysis per specimen.
- **Evidence** — your own photographs or diagrams, a non-food analogue, or the
  supplied evidence pack. Any mix of the three is acceptable.
- **Route** — one file, submitted to the SLOP2895 submission point before the
  deadline.

## The brief

An assembled sandwich is a structure that has already been through most of its
service life by the time you meet it. It has been built, held, set down,
carried, and left to stand. What is in front of you is the record of that
history, written in compression marks, migrated moisture, displaced layers and
separated interfaces — and almost none of it is labelled.

The declared problem is that untrained observation merges three different kinds
of statement into one. *The lower layer is saturated* is an observation. *The
sauce was applied to the lower layer* is an inference drawn from it. *How long
the specimen stood before you saw it* is, in most cases, simply unknown. Written
as one sentence, the three become indistinguishable, and a reader has no way to
tell which part of your account the evidence actually supports. Every later
assessment in this course depends on that distinction holding, so this is where
it is built.

You are not being asked to explain your specimens correctly. You are being asked
to record them in a form where a second person could reach a different
conclusion from the same evidence and see exactly where the two of you diverge.
Taste, ingredient cost, culinary skill and visual appeal are not assessed, and
no specimen needs to be prepared, purchased or eaten to be recorded.

## Required submission

Four entries. Each entry contains, in this order:

1. An annotated photograph or diagram of the specimen, with the layers,
   interfaces and any visible failure marked and labelled.
2. A structural classification of the specimen, stating the criterion the
   classification rests on.
3. One observed failure, or one credible failure risk, identified on the
   specimen itself.
4. Three separated statements headed **Observed**, **Inferred** and
   **Unknown**.
5. No more than 250 words of analysis.

## Recommended process

1. **Declare** the participation route and the evidence you hold for each
   specimen before you write anything about it.
2. **Observe** the specimen and record only what is present in the evidence:
   geometry, layer order, condition at each interface, visible deformation.
3. **Classify** the specimen, then state the criterion that decided it and one
   specimen the same criterion would classify awkwardly.
4. **Document** the failure or failure risk at the interface where it appears,
   not at the layer you suspect caused it.
5. **Compare** your four entries and check that a term used in one means the
   same thing in the others.
6. **Revise** any sentence that carries an observation and an explanation
   together, splitting it into the two statements it is actually making.

## Evidence standard

Each entry must keep three registers apart.

**Observed** is what a second person could confirm from your photograph,
diagram or supplied case without asking you a question. It is written in the
present tense and describes condition, not cause.

**Inferred** is what you conclude from the observation. Each inference names the
observation it rests on. An inference with no observation behind it is an
assertion and scores as one.

**Unknown** is what the evidence cannot settle: assembly order you did not see,
elapsed time, handling history, conditions before the specimen reached you. A
full Unknown statement is a strength in this task, not an admission. The common
failure is a log with an empty Unknown column and an inference doing its work
silently.

## Equivalent participation

Three routes are available, and all three assess the same outcome:

- **Physical specimen** — observe and record a real assembly, with no
  requirement to consume it.
- **Non-food analogue** — build and record the same structure in foam, sponge,
  cardboard, clay or textile, and classify it on the same criteria.
- **Supplied evidence** — work from the course photograph, measurement and
  video-frame packs.

No route loses marks. A log built entirely from supplied evidence can receive
full marks, because what is assessed is the quality of the observation and the
discipline of the separation, not the provenance of the specimen. See
[the participation policies](/policies/).

## Safety, cost and waste

Nothing in this task requires you to prepare, purchase, handle or consume food.
Use the smallest specimen that answers the question, and do not present a tested
specimen to anyone as safe to eat. Photographs of identifiable people or private
spaces need consent; the supplied packs exist so that field photography is never
compulsory. Full detail is on [the policies page](/policies/).
