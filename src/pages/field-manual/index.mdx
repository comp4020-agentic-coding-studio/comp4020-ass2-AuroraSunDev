> **Measure first. Explain second.**

This manual holds the methods, the vocabulary and the evidence standards used
across all twelve weeks and all four assessments, so that a week's brief can
name a method instead of restating it. It is a reference, not a reading. Every
week page and every assessment brief links to the section it uses; where a
brief and this page disagree on method, tell a member of staff so one of the
two can be corrected. The conditions under which any test may be run are set
by [the participation policies](/policies/), which govern this page.

## System vocabulary

These nine terms are used in one sense throughout the course. A record that
uses them in another sense is not wrong so much as unreadable by a second
person.

</div>

| Term | Definition in this course |
| --- | --- |
| Specimen | The whole assembly under test, identified before anything is applied to it. One specimen, one identifier, one record. |
| Component | One named material within a specimen, considered on its own. A component has properties; a specimen has behaviour. |
| Boundary | The surface at which the specimen meets its surroundings, including every exposed edge. The shortest route in for liquid, and a cut creates more of it. |
| Interface | The surface between two components. Where load is transferred between layers, and where grip is either held or lost. |
| Load | Any declared condition applied to a specimen that changes what it can carry: force, moisture, temperature, motion, or elapsed time. |
| Support | Anything that carries load out of the specimen — a bench, a hand, packaging, or an adjacent component. Named and positioned, or the reading means nothing. |
| Failure | The declared failure criterion has been met. Stated against that criterion and its time, never as an impression of the specimen. |
| Recovery | The geometry the specimen returns to once the load is removed. Measured on unloading, not assumed from the loaded state. |
| Load case | The written statement of loads, durations and sequence a specimen is to be tested against. Declared before the test, not after it. |

## The four failure families

The course sorts recurring failures into four families. Each is anchored in
one week, and every later week reuses the vocabulary rather than inventing its
own.

</div>

| Family | What moves | First visible sign | Where it is taught |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Slip | One component travels laterally across an interface. | A component edge sits outside the footprint of the one beneath it. | [Week 3: Grip, Friction and Slip](/sessions/week-03/) |
| Soak | A moisture front advances from a boundary into a porous layer. | Local darkening at the boundary, ahead of any change in shape. | [Week 4: Moisture Is a Load](/sessions/week-04/) |
| Crush | The stack loses height under compression. | A gap that carried the stack's own height has closed. | [Week 2: Bread Is a Beam](/sessions/week-02/) |
| Splay | An unbraced exposed edge opens outward. | Contents visible past the boundary line, with the stack still at height. | [Week 5: Load-Bearing Fillings](/sessions/week-05/) |

<div class="slop-measure">

**These four families are this course's own simplified analytical model.** They
were designed for SLOP2895 so that failures recorded in twelve different
investigations can be compared in one vocabulary. They are not a recognised
professional classification, they appear in no standard, and they must not be
cited as one — not in an assessment, and not outside the course.

Treat a family name as a comparison label and nothing more. A claim about what
a material or an interface did rests on the observation behind it; if the
observation is removed, the family name carries no evidence on its own. A
specimen may also sit in two families at once, and often does: a front that
reaches an interface produces Soak and then Slip, and saying so is more
defensible than choosing one.

## Observation protocol

Seven steps, in this order, for every test in the course. The order is the
method: steps one and two are what make step six possible.

1. **Identify.** Give the specimen an identifier and record what it is made of,
   in stack order, before anything is applied. An unidentified specimen cannot
   be compared with a second one.
2. **Condition.** State the condition the specimen is held in, and list what
   you are holding constant — dimensions, stack order, ambient temperature,
   support positions, span, and who is reading the instrument. A constant you
   did not name is a variable you did not control.
3. **Load.** Apply one declared load, at a stated magnitude, for a stated
   duration, in the same way each time. One load per test. If two conditions
   changed, the comparison has nothing to attribute the difference to.
4. **Observe.** Watch the specimen while the load is on it. Record the moment
   behaviour changes, not only the state you find at the end.
5. **Record.** Write each measurement with its unit, the location on the
   specimen it was taken from, and the elapsed time. A measurement without its
   time and place is not evidence in this course.
6. **Compare.** Set the reading against a control, or against another declared
   condition. A single reading describes a specimen; it establishes nothing.
7. **Interpret.** State what the comparison supports, then state what it does
   not. Separate observed, inferred and unknown before writing a cause.

## Test methods

Six methods. Each one states what it varies, what it measures, and what it
cannot establish — the third of those is the part that gets left out, and the
part that is marked.

### Compression

Varies the applied load, in steps, at a fixed span with fixed support
positions. Measures central deflection at each load step, and the geometry
recovered on unloading. Cannot establish the strength of a material: the
reading belongs to that specimen at that span with those supports, and a
shorter span on the same material gives a different answer. It cannot say when
a layer will hinge under a load it was not taken to. Used at
[Week 2: Bread Is a Beam](/sessions/week-02/), reused wherever a stack is
loaded at [Week 5: Load-Bearing Fillings](/sessions/week-05/) and
[Week 8: The Order of Layers](/sessions/week-08/), and the readiest variable
for the [Component Test](/assessments/component-test/).

### Slippage

Varies the surface pairing at one interface, and the normal load pressing that
pairing together. Measures the angle of first movement on a tilted support, and
the displacement travelled from it. Cannot establish a coefficient belonging to
one material: resistance is a property of the pair, under that normal load, at
that moment, and a pairing that exchanges moisture across its interface will
not give the same figure twenty minutes later. Nor does it locate which of the
two surfaces is responsible without a residue reading. Taught at
[Week 3: Grip, Friction and Slip](/sessions/week-03/), diagnosed at the film
scale in [Week 6: Sauce at the Interface](/sessions/week-06/), and accepted as
a declared variable in the [Component Test](/assessments/component-test/).

### Moisture migration

Varies elapsed time before observation, together with one declared boundary
condition — the position of the moist component in the stack, or the presence
of a barrier at an interface. Measures penetration depth of the visible front
from the boundary, read at stated elapsed times, against a dry control held
alongside.

Cannot establish why the front advanced, whether it reached an interface at
which it is not visible, or which structural effect accounts for a failure.
One front can soften a layer, swell it, or lubricate an interface, and a depth
reading does not separate the three.

On the model: the standard capillary relation for a porous medium
(Lucas–Washburn) gives penetration proportional to the square root of elapsed
time, under idealising assumptions — a rigid, uniform, non-swelling pore
network and a constant contact angle. A real outer layer satisfies none of
them. Read the shape of the advance, which is the transferable part; do not fit
a curve to it, and do not draw the intervals between readings you did not take.
Taught at [Week 4: Moisture Is a Load](/sessions/week-04/) with the full method
in [the Week 4 lecture](/lectures/moisture-is-a-load/), met again from inside
the assembly at [Week 7: Thermal Conflict](/sessions/week-07/), and carried
into the [Component Test](/assessments/component-test/).

### Thermal comparison

Varies nothing in the specimen. One condition is observed at three declared
time points against an ambient control, and nothing is observed between them.
Measures a temperature and the location it was taken from, the condition of the
inner face of the outer layer, and where liquid is visible, at each time point.
Cannot establish a temperature for the assembly — a gradient has no single
value, only a value and a place — and cannot separate a change caused by heat
from one caused by twenty minutes elapsing, unless the ambient control was run.
The course treats the assembly as three lumped thermal zones, which is a
teaching simplification and not a claim about how heat distributes. Taught at
[Week 7: Thermal Conflict](/sessions/week-07/), and the first artefact with
times on it for the [Failure Autopsy](/assessments/failure-autopsy/).

### Cutting geometry

**This method issues no cutting instruction and holds no blades for general
use.** It works from pre-cut stock supplied by the laboratory, from the
station's cut-path diagrams, or with equipment operated by a demonstrator on
request, and the same three routes are available at every station under
[the participation policies](/policies/).

Varies cut angle across a fixed specimen width. Measures exposed-face length at
each angle, and the span and support positions before and after the cut, marked
together on one section drawing. Cannot establish anything about how a cut was
made or what a blade did on its way through: the method treats the cut as
geometrically clean, with no crushing and no drag, which no real cut is. That
simplification is what allows geometry to be studied by itself, and it is a
limitation to declare rather than to leave implied. Taught at
[Week 9: Cutting Geometry](/sessions/week-09/), and it sets which sequences
were available to a specimen in the
[Failure Autopsy](/assessments/failure-autopsy/).

### Transport disturbance

Varies the route: a tilt held at a recorded angle, a compression load applied
through the packaging rather than directly to a layer, and a recorded number of
pick-ups, in fixed order. Measures the specimen's condition at each station,
photographed twice — in its packaging and out of it — against its own
pre-route record. Cannot establish which station produced a displacement when
only the end state was photographed, and establishes nothing at all about the
interior from a packaged photograph alone. Taught at
[Week 10: Packaging Is Part of the Structure](/sessions/week-10/), and applied
to a declared route in [The Sandwich Must Hold](/assessments/final/) and
[Week 12: The Final Stress Test](/sessions/week-12/).

## Evidence records

Six record types are accepted as evidence. Each has a threshold: a record
missing what its row names is a description, and is marked as one.

</div>

| Record | What it must carry to count |
| --- | --- |
| Annotated image | The specimen identifier, the elapsed time, the layers and interfaces labelled, and the feature being claimed marked on the specimen itself rather than described beside it. |
| Measurement table | One row per condition, the control included; a unit on every column; the location each reading was taken from; and the elapsed time of each reading. |
| Sequence diagram | Ordered steps with times or frame numbers, each step keyed to the evidence item supporting it, and a branch drawn wherever the evidence admits two orders. |
| Comparison | At least two conditions differing in one declared variable, the constants listed, and the control shown at the same size and detail as the conditions. |
| Limitation | What the method could not reach, stated as a property of the method rather than as an apology, and the claim it therefore rules out. |
| Uncertainty statement | The specific readings you doubt, why you doubt them, and the observation that would settle each. A reading recorded as uncertain is evidence; the same reading recorded as fact is not. |

## Observed, inferred, unknown

One packaged specimen, photographed before a route trace and again on arrival.
The two lengths below are illustrative course values, not measurements.

</div>

| Observed | Inferred | Unknown |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The packaging retained its outer geometry on arrival. | The packaging was not loaded past what it could carry along the route. | Whether it braced the assembly or only enclosed it. |
| On opening, the upper component sat about 9 mm outside the footprint of the one beneath it. | An interface transferred less shear than the route demanded of it. | Which of the three route stations the movement happened at. |
| The lower outer layer stood about 2 mm shorter than in its pre-route record. | The stack carried compression through the packaging rather than around it. | Whether the height loss preceded the displacement, followed it, or is unrelated to it. |

<div class="slop-measure">

Observed is what a second person could read off your record without your help.
Inferred is what you conclude from it, and it belongs in its own column because
it is arguable — a reader who disagrees needs somewhere to disagree. Unknown is
the evidence you do not have, and naming it is what stops an inference being
read as a measurement.

The failure this separation prevents is not a wrong answer. It is a confident
one: an inference written into the observation column, which removes the
reader's ability to test it. Assessment work is marked on the separation as
much as on the finding, from the [Sandwich Field Log](/assessments/field-log/)
onward.

## Failure notation

One symbol set, used on every diagram, table and sheet in the course. Plain
text throughout, so a record can be written by hand, typed, or copied into a
message without losing its meaning.

</div>

| Notation | Means | Used for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `t = 0` | The instant the declared load is applied. | Fixing the origin of every timed sequence. |
| `t = n` | Elapsed time, n minutes after `t = 0`. | Time-stamping each reading in a timed record. |
| `δ` | Displacement or deflection, in millimetres, at a stated location. | Compression and slippage records. |
| `θ` | Angle, in degrees. | Ramp angle at first movement; cut angle from the perpendicular. |
| `d` | Penetration depth of a moisture front from the boundary, in millimetres. | Moisture migration readings. |
| `A/B` | The interface between component A and component B. | Naming where a failure sits, rather than which layer it is near. |
| `C` | The control condition. | Marking the control row or station in any comparison. |
| `→` | Leads to. One step of a proposed sequence. | Sequence diagrams and causal chains. |
| `?` | Not established by this record. | Any step, order or value the evidence does not decide. |
| `[ ]` | No reading taken at this point. | Intervals between measurements, which are left blank rather than drawn. |
| `X` | The point at which the declared failure criterion was first met, located on the specimen. | Annotated images and failure diagrams. |

## Method limits

Every method on this page supports comparative reasoning inside a declared
condition. That is the whole of what it supports.

On this evidence a student **may** claim that one condition behaved differently
from another under the declared load, in the direction the record shows; that a
named layer, interface or edge is where a change first became visible; that a
proposed sequence is consistent with the evidence, or that the evidence does
not decide between two sequences; and that a reading is uncertain, together
with what would settle it.

On this evidence a student **may not** claim a material property, a
coefficient, a strength or a keeping time as a general value; that a result
holds outside the condition declared for it; that a specimen is safe, unsafe,
hygienic, or fit to eat; that a failure family is an established
classification; or that the curve between two readings is data.

None of this is professional engineering analysis. The rigs are teaching
instruments, the quoted figures across the site are illustrative course values,
and no result here transfers to a load-bearing, structural or regulated
application. Neither is any of it food-safety certification: no method on this
page measures contamination, microbial growth or fitness for consumption, and
nothing in the course produces a finding about them. Food safety is handled as
participation policy in [the participation policies](/policies/), where it
governs what may be run at all — a structural result never overrides a
food-safety requirement.
