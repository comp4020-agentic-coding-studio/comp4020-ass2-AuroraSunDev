# Moisture Is a Load

## SLOP2895 · Week 4

**How does liquid progressively compromise a boundary?**

```notes
Week 2 loaded the outer layer mechanically. This week nothing is pressed and
nothing is lifted. The specimen is left alone on the bench, and it still fails.
```

## Moisture Is a Load

<SlideFigure figure="instrumented" />

Calling it a load is a modelling decision: declare it, apply it under a stated
condition, measure what it does.

```notes
Slide 1. The title is a claim, not a metaphor. Treating moisture as a load
means treating it the way the course treats any other load.
```

---

## The Failure Arrives Late

<SlideFigure figure="late" />

A compression failure happens while you watch. This one does not.

```notes
Slide 2. The specimen that fails is the specimen you tested, plus a delay.

Every record this week carries its elapsed time. A measurement without a time
is an anecdote with a number in it.
```

---

## What Is the Boundary Doing?

<SlideFigure figure="boundary" />

The layer, the interface and the exposed edge are three different things.

```notes
Slide 3. When a record says "the boundary failed", ask which one.

Liquid travels *through* a layer, *along* an interface, and straight in at an
exposed edge. Three findings, three remedies.
```

---

## A Simplified Moisture-Front Model

<SlideFigure figure="front" />

One measurable quantity: penetration depth, at a stated time.

```notes
Slide 4. The values are illustrative course values.

What matters is the shape: fast, then slower. That is the square-root-of-time
behaviour the standard capillary model predicts, and it is why doubling the
delay does not double the damage.

The model records what changes and where. Not why — that is the next slide.
```

---

## Soften, Swell, Lubricate

<SlideFigure figure="three-effects" />

One front. Three possible structural effects, and they need different
revisions.

```notes
Slide 5. Softening is a stiffness problem. Swelling is a geometry problem.
Lubrication is a slip problem.

A specimen can show all three. The question is which one accounts for the
failure you observed — and whether your evidence can tell them apart at all.
```

---

## Observation Is Not Cause

<SlideFigure figure="oiu" />

```notes
Slide 6. The failure mode here is not a wrong answer. It is a confident answer:
a record that writes the inference into the observation column and so removes
the reader's ability to disagree with it.
```

## Designing a Controlled Comparison

<SlideFigure figure="bench" />

One declared variable. Three conditions. One dry control.

```notes
Slide 7. Then list what you held constant: dimensions, layer order, ambient
temperature, elapsed time at reading, and who read it.

A constant you did not name is a variable you did not control, and the marker
will find it before you do.
```

---

## Reading the Evidence

<SlideFigure figure="plot" />

Read the order, the shape and the control. Do not read the points you did not
measure.

```notes
Slide 8. The curve between two readings is a drawing convention, not data. This
plot leaves those intervals dashed on purpose, and student plots should too.

If the dry control also deformed, part of what you attributed to moisture
belongs to something else.
```

---

## Competing Explanations

<SlideFigure figure="branch" />

Two branches stay live. Nothing recorded separates them.

```notes
Slide 9. A good autopsy does not pick the most familiar cause. It states all of
them, says which recorded evidence rules any out, and leaves the rest live.

Week 11 is built entirely on doing this well.
```

---

## Investigation Brief

<SlideFigure figure="protocol" />

Three equivalent routes. The same artefact leaves the session.

```notes
Slide 10. Physical specimen, never eaten. Non-food analogue — sponge and card
read more cleanly than the real thing, because their pore structure is more
uniform. Or the supplied evidence pack, with timed photographs and measurements
from a prepared comparison.
```

---

## Check Your Diagnosis

<SlideFigure figure="case" />

Which families, and what is missing?

```notes
Slide 11. Take a minute before revealing.

Soak reaching an interface and producing Slip. Not Crush: nothing lost height.
The order of events is not established, because no intermediate observation was
taken — and that sentence is the part worth marks.

Say out loud: Slip, Soak, Crush and Splay are this course's own analytical
categories. They are not a recognised professional classification.
```

## From Week 4 to Component Test

<SlideFigure figure="path" />

One declared variable, three conditions, a protocol, a comparison, an
interpretation and its limits.

```notes
Slide 12. That is the structure of slides 7 and 8.

Moisture is the most tractable variable to isolate, because the front is
visible and the measurement is a length. It is not the only one: interface grip
and stiffness category are equally legitimate, and Weeks 5 and 6 supply both.

Before next week: bring the absorption observation, with uncertain readings
marked uncertain.
```
